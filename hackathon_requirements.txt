1-Day Hackathon — Elaborated Problem Statements
Scoped for a 1–2 hour build window, for 3rd/4th-year engineering participants. Judging rubric weighted toward Systems Thinking and AI-First Problem Solving.
How to Make This a Great Hackathon Entry
Format & Scope
Total time: 60–90 minutes to build, 10–15 minutes to prepare the pitch, ~5 minutes per team to present (2 min problem framing, 3 min demo + ask).
A working prototype is a script, spreadsheet, clickable mockup, or scripted conversation flow — not a production app. Static or sample data is expected and fine.
AI-assisted coding tools (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Voiceflow, Bubble, etc.) are encouraged. Speed of assembly is part of the AI-first judging criterion.
What Makes an Entry Effective
Depth on one narrow slice beats breadth across the whole problem — judges reward a sharp, working piece over an ambitious but unfinished system.
Show your reasoning, not just your output. A visible 'why' (a rule, a scoring logic, a named psychological principle) is what separates a toy from a credible prototype.
Name at least one thing your prototype gets wrong or doesn't handle. Self-awareness about limits reads as maturity, not weakness.
How to Present
Open with the business situation in one or two sentences before touching the build — judges should understand *why this matters* before they see *what you made*.
Demo live if possible, even with 3–5 sample inputs typed in front of the judges. A live (if small) demo beats a polished recorded walkthrough.
Close with the metric your solution is meant to move, and how you'd know if it actually worked — not just that it runs.

Track — AI Systems & Product
  Sample 1 — The Invisible Bottleneck
Industry: D2C / E-commerce customer support
Business Situation: A growing D2C brand (think: skincare, electronics accessories) has scaled from 500 to 5,000 orders a day. An 8-person support team is buried in tickets — most repetitive (order status, return policy) but a meaningful share are emotionally charged (damaged product, delayed refund) where a bot response makes things worse, not better.
The Business Problem / Opportunity: Leadership already tried a generic chatbot. CSAT dropped and escalations rose, because every query, simple or sensitive, got funneled through the bot first. The real opportunity isn't 'add AI' — it's building the judgment layer that decides where AI genuinely helps versus where it actively damages trust.
What to Build (in 1–2 hours)
A notebook or spreadsheet that takes 10–15 sample support queries and classifies each as 'AI can handle' or 'Needs human,' with a one-line reason per query.
Suggested Tools: ChatGPT/Claude directly, or an API call in a short Python script. Output as a simple table.
What Makes It Effective
The classification logic is visible and defensible — not just a label, but a reason a support manager would trust. Bonus: one query the prototype deliberately gets 'wrong,' with an honest explanation of why.
How to Present It
Show the queries, the classifications, and reasoning as a table. Close by live-testing one new query the judges suggest, and narrate the reasoning out loud as it classifies.
Best-Practice Tip
Pull real queries from actual brand complaints on Twitter/Reddit for authenticity — invented queries tend to feel too clean and too easy to classify.
  Sample 2 — Build vs. Buy, Decided by AI
Industry: SaaS / early-stage startup product teams
Business Situation: A 40-person startup's product team is debating whether to build an in-house analytics dashboard or license a tool like Mixpanel. Every decision like this currently takes 6–8 weeks of Slack debate, competing priorities, and whoever argues loudest in the room.
The Business Problem / Opportunity: The real cost isn't the tool — it's decision paralysis and an inconsistent process that depends on who's in the room. Smaller teams without a dedicated strategy function feel this most acutely. There's an opportunity to standardize how these calls get made and made faster.
What to Build (in 1–2 hours)
A scoring calculator that takes 3–4 real constraint inputs (cost, timeline, tech debt, integration risk) and outputs a Build/Buy recommendation with visible, inspectable reasoning.
Suggested Tools: A Google Sheet with weighted formulas, optionally wrapped with one LLM call that turns the score into a plain-language recommendation.
What Makes It Effective
The tool shows its reasoning trail, not just a verdict, and demonstrates sensitivity — changing one input changes the recommendation in a way judges can follow.
How to Present It
Live demo: run 2–3 different scenarios in front of judges and show the recommendation (and reasoning) shift as inputs change. This sensitivity demo is what separates a credible tool from a gimmick.
Best-Practice Tip
Use trade-off categories judges already recognize (cost, time, lock-in) rather than invented jargon — the logic should be instantly legible.
  Sample 3 — The Feedback Loop No One Closes
Industry: Consumer app / SaaS product teams
Business Situation: A consumer app with 50,000 users generates hundreds of App Store reviews, support tickets, and NPS comments every month. The product team currently skims a handful of reviews manually before each sprint planning meeting.
The Business Problem / Opportunity: High-signal feedback gets buried under repeated, low-value complaints. The opportunity is converting unstructured, scattered feedback into a ranked, defensible product priority list — the kind a Head of Product could act on directly.
What to Build (in 1–2 hours)
A script or notebook that takes 15–20 sample feedback snippets and groups them into themes, ranked by frequency and severity.
Suggested Tools: One LLM prompt to tag and cluster the feedback, or basic keyword grouping in Python if working offline.
What Makes It Effective
The output surfaces at least one quiet-but-important insight that a simple 'most common complaint' count would have missed — that's the signal judges are looking for.
How to Present It
Present as a before/after: raw scattered feedback on the left, ranked backlog with one-line justification per item on the right. Pitch it as if presenting to a Head of Product.
Best-Practice Tip
Use messy, realistic sample feedback — a mix of vague, contradictory, and specific comments. Overly clean sample data feels artificial to judges.
Track — Consumer Growth & Applied Psychology
  Sample 1 — The Loyalty Illusion
Industry: D2C retail / subscription
Business Situation: A coffee subscription brand runs a simple loyalty program: buy 9, get the 10th free. Everyone participates, but churn hasn't moved — customers redeem the reward and leave anyway.
The Business Problem / Opportunity: The company is optimizing for repeat purchase, not loyalty. There's a real difference between a customer buying again out of habit or sunk cost, versus buying again because they genuinely prefer the brand. The opportunity is designing a mechanism that builds real preference, not just repetition.
What to Build (in 1–2 hours)
A visual mockup (Figma, slides, or even hand-drawn and photographed) of one new loyalty mechanic, paired with a half-page brief on the psychology behind it.
Suggested Tools: Figma, Canva, or plain slides — no data or backend needed. This is a concept pitch with a visual anchor.
What Makes It Effective
A named psychological principle (variable reward, identity signaling, the endowment effect) is explicitly mapped onto the mechanic, and the proposed loyalty metric is one a pure discount program would fail.
How to Present It
Show the mockup, then walk through the psychology brief. Include a mock customer quote that shows the emotional hook in action — the reasoning is the pitch here, not the UI polish.
Best-Practice Tip
Reference one real example that 'gets it' — gaming loot mechanics, or a niche brand's cult following — as inspiration or contrast for your idea.
  Sample 2 — Friction as a Feature
Industry: E-commerce / fashion retail
Business Situation: An online fashion retailer has a return rate above 30%, driven largely by impulsive one-click purchases that customers regret within days.
The Business Problem / Opportunity: Removing all friction maximized short-term conversion but quietly hurt margins through return logistics. The opportunity is a deliberately placed pause that reduces impulsive buying without meaningfully hurting overall conversion.
What to Build (in 1–2 hours)
A before/after wireframe of one specific step in the journey (checkout or cancellation), showing exactly where friction was added and how it appears to the user.
Suggested Tools: Figma, Canva, or simple slides showing two screens — no working code required.
What Makes It Effective
A clear justification for why that specific step (not another) was chosen, plus a stated prediction: which metric should improve, and which might dip, and why that trade-off is worth it.
How to Present It
Walk through the two screens, then explain the psychological principle behind the pause (commitment device, reflective delay) and the trade-off being made.
Best-Practice Tip
Pick one step, not a full redesign. Depth on a single moment beats a broad but shallow journey overhaul.
  Sample 3 — Word-of-Mouth, Engineered
Industry: Consumer app / D2C subscription
Business Situation: A meal-kit delivery startup runs a referral program (₹500 off) but referral volume has stayed flat despite decent customer satisfaction scores.
The Business Problem / Opportunity: Most people who do refer were probably going to anyway — the incentive is paying for advocacy that would have happened for free, not creating new advocacy. The opportunity is triggering the ask at the psychological peak moment (right after a great unboxing, for instance) instead of a generic post-purchase nudge.
What to Build (in 1–2 hours)
A mockup of one specific referral trigger moment (an in-app popup or WhatsApp message), plus a short note on how you'd isolate incremental referrals.
Suggested Tools: Figma/Canva for the mockup; a short written note (not a working test) describing a proposed A/B or holdout comparison.
What Makes It Effective
The submission clearly separates incremental advocacy from advocacy that would have happened anyway, and shows exactly where in the user journey the trigger fires.
How to Present It
Show the exact screen/moment where the ask appears, explain the behavioral trigger by name, and walk through how you'd measure whether it actually created new referrals.
Best-Practice Tip
Show the moment, not just the mechanic. A screenshot of precisely when and where this fires is more convincing than a description of the idea in the abstract.
Track — Clinical & HealthTech
  Sample 1 — The Adherence Gap
Industry: Chronic disease management (health-tech app)
Business Situation: A diabetes care startup has 2,000 patients on its app. Medication and logging adherence drops by roughly 40% after week three.
The Business Problem / Opportunity: The team assumed patients simply forget, so they added reminders — adherence didn't improve. The real bottleneck may be something else entirely: cost anxiety, symptom-free complacency, or the logistics of refills. The opportunity is correctly diagnosing the true bottleneck for one condition and targeting that, specifically.
What to Build (in 1–2 hours)
A short diagnosis memo (half a page) naming the real bottleneck, paired with a scripted nudge flow (a WhatsApp-style conversation on slides, or a simple Voiceflow chatbot) targeted at that specific cause.
Suggested Tools: Slides or a basic Voiceflow/chatbot builder for the scripted flow — no real patient data or integration needed.
What Makes It Effective
The strength is in the diagnosis, not the intervention. Judges should see a reasoned case for why this is the real bottleneck, not a generic assumption dressed up as insight.
How to Present It
Present the memo first — the 'why' — then the scripted intervention as the 'what.' Keep the intervention tightly scoped to the diagnosed cause.
Best-Practice Tip
Ground the chosen bottleneck in something specific and plausible — cite a known adherence statistic or pattern if you can, rather than asserting a generic cause.
  Sample 2 — The Second Opinion Problem
Industry: Diagnostics / telehealth
Business Situation: A telehealth platform sends lab results to patients through a portal with minimal explanation, which leads to anxious calls and confusion, especially for borderline or ambiguous values.
The Business Problem / Opportunity: Patients need clarity without being falsely alarmed or falsely reassured — a communication problem as much as an information one. The opportunity is an explanation layer that helps patients understand next steps without replacing the doctor's judgment.
What to Build (in 1–2 hours)
A prompt-based tool that takes 2–3 sample (clearly fabricated) ambiguous lab results and explains them in plain language, with an explicit 'this is not a diagnosis, talk to your doctor' boundary built in.
Suggested Tools: ChatGPT/Claude with a carefully written prompt; present as a scripted or live demo.
What Makes It Effective
The tool handles at least one genuinely ambiguous result well and clearly states its own limits — treat the disclaimer as a designed feature, not a caveat to bury in fine print.
How to Present It
Enter a sample result live, show the explanation output, then explain how you tested the tone against both an anxious-patient framing and a dismissive-patient framing.
Best-Practice Tip
Fabricate lab data and label it clearly as fictional. Never use real patient data, and never imply clinical validation you don't have.
  Sample 3 — The Referral Black Hole
Industry: Hospital networks / multi-provider healthcare systems
Business Situation: A multi-specialty hospital chain refers roughly 15% of patients to specialists. Internal estimates suggest a third of those referrals never get completed — lost to scheduling friction, cost concerns, or simply being forgotten.
The Business Problem / Opportunity: No one currently owns the referral after it's issued — it disappears into the gap between departments or systems. The opportunity is a lightweight tracker that makes referral status visible and prompts action at the right moment, before it goes stale.
What to Build (in 1–2 hours)
A tracker (Google Sheet or simple script) with 5–10 sample referrals moving through stages, using conditional formatting or basic logic to flag referrals stuck too long.
Suggested Tools: Google Sheets with conditional formatting, or a short Python script simulating the same logic.
What Makes It Effective
The tracker visibly catches at least one referral before it would have gone stale, and the submission defines a completion metric distinct from simple referral volume.
How to Present It
Walk through the sheet/dashboard with sample referrals in different stages, color-coded by drop-off risk, and explain the nudge logic behind the flagging.
Best-Practice Tip
Focus on one handoff point in the referral chain rather than trying to model the entire hospital system end to end.
